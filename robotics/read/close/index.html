What you can expect after six months

What you can expect after these 6 months???

I am going to be honest, because the honest version is the only version worth reading

This roadmap will not make you a senior robotics engineer in six months

Senior in this field means five or more years of deploying real-time systems on physical robots, and every job listing I checked says so explicitly

What it will make you is someone who can build, debug and ship working robots, who understands the stack from the electrical layer up to the learned policy, and who has the physical portfolio to prove it

That is enough to get you into the field, and the field is where the compounding happens

The demand picture, honestly

I want to give you the real numbers rather than the recruiting-blog numbers, because several widely-quoted robotics statistics do not survive checking

The capital is real. Physical AI startups raised $47.4B in H1 2026 across 521 deals, more than 2022 to 2024 combined, per Crunchbase. Narrower robotics-only funding hit $18.8B by late June 2026, against $15B for all of 2025

The individual rounds are real. Neura Robotics raised $1.4B, Skild AI $1.4B, Apptronik $520M, and Figure carries a $39B post-money valuation per The Robot Report's 2026 outlook

But hiring is lagging the capital, and you should know that. North American robot orders grew only 2.0% in units in H1 2026 per A3. US installations fell 9% in 2024 per the IFR. The BLS occupational code containing robotics engineers projects 1 to 2% growth, not the 10% figure you will find quoted in career articles with no source behind it

And robotics is a much smaller labour market than software. BLS projects 106,100 annual openings for software developers against roughly 57,200 for mechanical, electrical and industrial engineers combined

So the accurate claim is not "robotics is booming, everyone will get hired"

It is that money is arriving faster than qualified people are, that the shortage is concentrated in specific specialisms, and that the entry tier is unusually open to people without degrees

One number that makes that concrete: the largest robotics job category by posting volume is Automation and Robotics Technician at 20.3% of 3,113 postings, and 68% of robotics technician roles are satisfied by an associate's degree or a certificate per O*NET

What it pays

Full-time in the US, and note that sources disagree substantially, so I am giving you the range and the source rather than one flattering number:

  • O*NET/BLS median: $122,930 for robotics engineers, which is the only official figure here
  • Entry level: roughly $80k to $100k per Payscale and Salary.com, higher at coastal and well-funded employers
  • Mid level, 3 to 6 years: $120k to $165k at conventional employers
  • Senior: $160k to $230k mainstream
  • Frontier physical-AI companies pay well above that. A live Figure listing for Helix AI Engineer, Robot Learning posts $200,000 to $400,000 base, and their perception role posts $200,000 to $350,000
  • Robot foundation model specialists: $280k to $475k total comp per recruiter placement data, though that is recruiter-reported rather than survey data and is heavily equity-weighted

Outside the US, to calibrate: Germany around €70,000 median total per Glassdoor, Switzerland around CHF 95,000, UK base £32,000 to £50,000, India around ₹653,000

Freelance and contract

  • Robotics engineers on Upwork list $22 to $70/hour, clustering $30 to $50
  • ROS project work on Upwork is priced per deliverable: $500 to $1,200 for a node implementation, $1,200 to $2,500 for launch configuration, $4,500 to $7,000 for a sensor integration pipeline, $7,000 to $12,000 for a full system architecture
  • ZipRecruiter puts ROS developers at an average $52.84/hour

And the number that makes integration a career rather than a task: in a robot cell, the robot itself averages about 25% of the total project cost. The other three quarters is engineering, safety and integration. A $35k arm becomes an $80k working system, and that $45k gap is what you are selling

Entry points that need no degree and no experience

  • Teleoperation and data collection averages $28.24/hour, with most between $22.12 and $32.93. Figure posted a Humanoid Robot Operator role at $25 to $35/hour with no degree requirement stated, and Weave Robotics posted at $25/hour
  • Robotics technician median $73,900 per BLS, typically entering with an associate's degree

Be clear-eyed about the first one: that pay band is mostly in the most expensive metros in the US, often on-site and physically demanding. It is a genuine foot in the door at frontier companies, and it is not a comfortable living in San Francisco

Now here is what I actually want you to take away from all of this

Build the projects, do not read about them. Pick one build from each month and actually make it. The line follower, the balancing robot, the arm, the ROS 2 navigation stack, the trained policy. Break them, fix them, film them, push them to GitHub. Every recruiter source I checked said the same thing: portfolio-first sourcing beats keyword search, and the projects that get people hired are the ones with logged metrics and a visible debugging history

Write down what broke. This is the specific advice I would give if you only take one thing from this article. Anyone can post a working demo. Almost nobody documents the four things that failed first and how they diagnosed each one. That write-up is the closest thing to proof that you can actually engineer, and it is the reason your repo survives the third follow-up question in an interview

Start before you feel ready. Apply for the technician role, take the teleoperation shift, contribute the small fix to Nav2, message the founder of the three-person robotics startup. The gap between "I am learning robotics" and "I work in robotics" is almost entirely a gap of nerve, and nobody in this field will ever tell you that you have studied enough

Six months of two focused hours a day is enough to change what you are able to do with your hands and your head

The hardware is cheaper than it has ever been, the software is open, the courses are free, and the people who can actually make machines work are genuinely scarce

Go build something that moves
